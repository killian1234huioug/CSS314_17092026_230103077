## Executive Summary

This report presents measured results from five Python multiprocessing experiments: hardware and timer calibration, shared-memory false-sharing diagnosis, strong and weak scaling, empirical Roofline/GEMM benchmarking, and scheduling under a skewed workload.

The main observations were:

- The Apple M3 system reported 8 logical CPUs, a 128-byte cache line, 64 KB L1 data cache, and 4 MB L2 cache.
- In Phase 1, increasing the separation between worker counters improved the measured stride-sweep time from 545.326 ms at stride 1 to 448.589 ms at stride 16. The trend was gradual rather than a sharp discontinuity.
- In Phase 2, strong scaling reached 4.8414× at 8 processes, with 60.52% parallel efficiency. Weak-scaling efficiency declined to 54.49% at 8 processes.
- In Phase 3, measured SharedMemory-copy bandwidth was 1.5788 GB/s and the Python compute ceiling was 0.4448 GFLOP/s. The best blocked tile among the six tested sizes was BS=128 at 0.061346 GFLOP/s. The numerical comparison passed with zero maximum absolute error.
- In Phase 4, cyclic static scheduling was fastest overall at 3.6804 s. Dynamic scheduling with C=1 had the lowest imbalance factor (1.0020), but its wall time was 3.7733 s.

## 3. Phase 1 — False Sharing and Cache-Line Contention

### 3.1 Stride sensitivity

Eight processes updated separate 64-bit slots in one shared-memory buffer. The stride is measured in 64-bit words; therefore, stride 16 corresponds to 16 × 8 = 128 bytes, equal to the reported Apple M3 cache-line size.

| Stride (64-bit words) | Separation (bytes) | Median (ms) | IQR (ms) | Throughput (Mops/s) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 8 | 545.326 | 96.405 | 29.340 |
| 2 | 16 | 497.695 | 41.728 | 32.148 |
| 4 | 32 | 468.502 | 31.223 | 34.151 |
| 8 | 64 | 467.517 | 37.840 | 34.223 |
| 16 | 128 | 448.589 | 15.181 | 35.667 |
| 32 | 256 | 466.452 | 56.075 | 34.301 |

The lowest median in the stride sweep was measured at stride 16 (128-byte separation). Compared with stride 1, the median decreased by approximately 17.7%. The data show a gradual improvement, not a single sharp drop at one exact offset. The result is consistent with reduced cache-line sharing when each worker's counter is separated by one 128-byte line.

### 3.2 Process scaling and Karp–Flatt metric

Speedup is calculated as \(S_p=T_1/T_p\), efficiency as \(E_p=S_p/p\), and the Karp–Flatt metric as:

\[
e(p)=\frac{1/S_p-1/p}{1-1/p},\quad p\geq 2.
\]

**Stride = 1:**

| Processes (p) | Median (ms) | Speedup | Efficiency | Karp–Flatt e(p) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2138.431 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | — |
| 2 | 1118.571 | 1.9118 | 0.9559 | 0.046161 |
| 4 | 637.791 | 3.3529 | 0.8382 | 0.064336 |
| 8 | 496.178 | 4.3098 | 0.5387 | 0.122319 |

**Stride = 16:**

| Processes (p) | Median (ms) | Speedup | Efficiency | Karp–Flatt e(p) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2134.063 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | — |
| 2 | 1123.003 | 1.9003 | 0.9502 | 0.052455 |
| 4 | 634.962 | 3.3609 | 0.8402 | 0.063382 |
| 8 | 453.718 | 4.7035 | 0.5879 | 0.100123 |

At eight processes, the stride-1 Karp–Flatt value (0.122319) is higher than the stride-16 value (0.100123). This indicates a larger effective serial/overhead component in the unpadded case. Both values increase as process count grows, showing that the measured system departs from ideal linear scaling. The padded layout reduces the measured penalty but does not remove process-management and other overheads.

**Interpretation:** On this Apple M3, 128-byte separation produced the best stride-sweep result and improved the eight-process scaling result. However, the stride sweep did not show a dramatic discontinuity exactly at stride 16; the report therefore avoids claiming a sharp cache-line cliff that the measurements do not demonstrate.

## 4. Phase 2 — Strong Scaling, Weak Scaling, and Amdahl/Gustafson Analysis

### 4.1 Strong scaling

The strong-scaling problem size was held constant at 1,200,000 elements. The measured median times and derived speedups are:

| Processes (p) | Median time (ms) | Speedup | Parallel efficiency |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|

| 3 | 879.056 | 2.7730 | 0.9243 |
| 4 | 715.531 | 3.4067 | 0.8517 |
| 5 | 628.888 | 3.8760 | 0.7752 |
| 6 | 570.398 | 4.2735 | 0.7122 |
| 7 | 553.317 | 4.4054 | 0.6293 |
| 8 | 503.484 | 4.8414 | 0.6052 |

The observed speedup at eight processes was 4.8414×, and efficiency was 60.52%.

The measured serial fraction was \(s=0.018365\). The Amdahl asymptotic limit is:

\[
S_{\infty}=\frac{1}{s}=54.4510.
\]

The theoretical Amdahl speedup at eight processes is approximately:

\[
S_8=\frac{1}{s+(1-s)/8}\approx 7.07.
\]

The measured 4.8414× speedup is below this idealized estimate. Amdahl's formula models a simplified fixed serial fraction and does not include all process scheduling, communication, interpreter, and heterogeneous-core effects.

## 5. Phase 3 — Empirical Roofline and GEMM Optimization

### 5.1 Empirical ceilings

| Measurement | Result |
|---|---:|
| Shared-memory copy median | 0.085013 s |
| Measured sustained bandwidth | 1.5788 GB/s |
| Compute benchmark median | 1.079132 s |
| Total compute operations | 480,000,000 FLOPs |
| Measured Python compute ceiling | 0.4448 GFLOP/s |
| Ridge point | 0.2817 FLOP/byte |

The ridge point is calculated as:

\[
I^*=\frac{P_{sustained}}{BW_{sustained}}=
\frac{0.4448\;\text{GFLOP/s}}{1.5788\;\text{GB/s}}
=0.2817\;\text{FLOP/byte}.
\]

These are empirical ceilings for the Python multiprocessing microbenchmarks. In particular, the compute test is not a native hardware FMA peak measurement; it includes Python interpreter and process execution overhead. The bandwidth test is based on SharedMemory slice copying and should not be interpreted as a vendor-specified maximum DRAM bandwidth.

### 5.2 GEMM variants

| Variant | Median time (s) | Performance (GFLOP/s) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Naive i-j-k | 0.473681 | 0.070838 |
| Reordered i-k-j | 0.552010 | 0.060786 |
| Blocked i-k-j, BS=128 | 0.546969 | 0.061346 |

The naive version was fastest in this measured Python implementation. Although loop reordering and blocking are common locality optimizations, they are not guaranteed to win for every small problem size or interpreter-level implementation. Process startup, Python loop overhead, and the particular matrix dimension can influence the outcome.

## 6. Phase 4 — Skewed Workloads and Scheduling

The experiment compared contiguous static blocks, cyclic round-robin assignment, and a synchronized dynamic shared counter. The load-imbalance factor is:

\[
\lambda=\frac{\max_t(t_{worker,t})}{\operatorname{mean}_t(t_{worker,t})}.
\]

A value near 1.0 indicates that active worker times are well balanced.

| Scheduling strategy | Wall time (s) | Imbalance factor (λ) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Contiguous Block Static | 6.9008 | 2.4094 |
| Cyclic Round-Robin Static | **3.6804** | 1.0370 |
| Dynamic C=1 | 3.7733 | **1.0020** |
| Dynamic C=8 | 3.7780 | 1.0060 |
| Dynamic C=64 | 3.9426 | 1.0671 |
| Dynamic C=512 | 5.1544 | 1.5217 |

Contiguous block scheduling performed poorly because the workload increased with the item index. The later workers received much more expensive items: Worker 0 was active for 0.0750 s, while Worker 7 was active for 6.8298 s. Cyclic scheduling distributed high- and low-cost items across workers and reduced the imbalance to 1.0370.

Dynamic C=1 achieved the lowest imbalance (1.0020), but the synchronized counter introduces coordination overhead. As chunk size increased, the number of claims decreased, but larger chunks made the tail of the workload less evenly distributed. C=512 had an imbalance of 1.5217 and took 5.1544 s.

Among the tested dynamic configurations, **C*=1** gave the best balance and the lowest dynamic wall time. Across all strategies, cyclic static scheduling was slightly faster (3.6804 s) than dynamic C=1 (3.7733 s), so it was the fastest overall in this run.

**Parameter note:** The executed configuration used N=8,000, K(i)=1+floor((i/40)^2), and dynamic chunks {1, 8, 64, 512}. The handout specifies N=12,000, K(i)=1+floor((i/60)^2), and chunks {1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024}. The report records the executed configuration.

## 7. Overall Conclusions

The experiments demonstrate that multiprocessing performance depends on memory layout, workload size, core topology, and scheduling policy—not only on the number of processes.

1. **False sharing:** Separating shared counters by 128 bytes improved the stride-sweep result and reduced the effective overhead indicated by the Karp–Flatt metric at eight processes. The measured trend was gradual, so the result supports reduced cache-line contention without proving a sharp boundary discontinuity.
2. **Scaling:** Strong scaling reached 4.8414× at eight processes, but efficiency fell to 60.52%. Weak-scaling efficiency also declined as the workload grew. The 4+4 P-core/E-core topology is a plausible contributor to the marginal-gain change beyond four processes.
3. **Roofline and GEMM:** The measured Python compute ceiling and SharedMemory bandwidth define an empirical Roofline for these specific tests. The best blocked tile was BS=128, but its advantage over BS=32 was small. The naive GEMM was fastest in this implementation, showing that theoretical locality improvements must be validated empirically.
4. **Scheduling:** Cyclic static scheduling gave the lowest overall wall time. Dynamic C=1 gave the best dynamic load balance, while large chunks caused a more uneven tail.

The results are specific to the tested MacBook Air M3, Python 3.9.6, macOS 26.2, and the executed benchmark configurations. They should not be treated as universal hardware limits.
